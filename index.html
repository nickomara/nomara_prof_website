About

Reading Earth's past to understand fire's future

I'm a paleoclimate scientist and biogeochemist studying how climate, fire, and ecosystems have shaped one another across Earth's recent past.

My research reconstructs the deep history of monsoons, wildfires, and savanna ecosystems in Northwest Africa using organic geochemistry and molecular biomarkers preserved in marine and lake sediments. By reading these natural archives, I trace how rainfall, vegetation, and fire regimes responded to past climate change — from glacial cycles and the Green Sahara to the human-influenced present — and what that history reveals about the future of fire on a warming planet.

I'm currently a Postdoctoral Research Associate in the Department of Ecology & Evolutionary Biology at Princeton University. Previously, I was the G.E. Hutchinson Postdoctoral Fellow at the Yale School of the Environment and the Yale Institute for Biospheric Studies. I earned my Ph.D. in Earth and Environmental Sciences at Columbia University and the Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory, and a B.S. in Geology–Chemistry from Brown University.

Research themes

What I study

Paleoclimate & monsoons

Reconstructing the strength and drivers of the West African monsoon across glacial cycles and the African Humid Period, and the orbital and ocean controls behind them.

Paleofire history

Developing molecular fire-biomarker proxies to chart how wildfire regimes in African savannas changed from the Last Glacial Maximum through the human-influenced present.

Ecosystem & vegetation change

Tracking shifts between forest, savanna, and desert using terrestrial plant biomarkers — and linking ecological transitions to climate, fire, and human activity.
